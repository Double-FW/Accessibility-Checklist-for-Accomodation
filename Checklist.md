# Accessible Living Quarters for Actors: Design Requirements Checklist

**Purpose.** Design the accommodation as one accessible journey: understanding the site, reaching a destination, using the room independently, and obtaining support when needed.  
<br/>Physical access (P),  
Non-visual/sensory access (N)  
Cognitive/neuroinclusive access (C).  
<br/>In England, use Gov.UK [Approved Document M](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/access-to-and-use-of-buildings-approved-document-m) as the regulatory baseline and BS 8300-1/-2 and PAS 6463 as best-practice references. The Equality Act 2010 can require reasonable adjustments beyond Building Regulations. Confirm the building's legal classification with Building Control.

## 1\. Knowing where you are

\[ \] (P/N/C) Provide an electronic accessible site map before arrival and on site, showing step-free routes, lifts (including instructions), toilets, quiet routes, rehearsal/stage destinations, drop-off points, assistance-dog relief areas and hints as to where accessible signage is located. Supply as accessible HTML/Word information as well as visual maps.

\[ \] (N/C) Use large, high-contrast, plain-language signs with consistent symbols and predictable positioning. Room numbers and key destinations should be consistent in format, including their positioning, and include easily locatable tactile/embossed information and Braille where appropriate (the sign is reachable and findable because the approach to positioning is consistently applied).

\[ \] (N/C) Use "heads-up" maps oriented in the direction the person is facing, with "you are here", landmarks and journey times. Consider tactile maps at key decision points.

\[ \] (N/C) Consider NaviLens markers at entrances, junctions, lifts, bathrooms, fire escapes and destinations for spoken, multilingual location information. Technology must supplement, not replace, physical wayfinding.

\[ \] (C/N) Identify zones on maps using colour plus pattern, words or symbols - not colour alone, and match those colours to ones used in signage or on key doors or walls in physical spaces. Keep naming, numbering and signage consistent.

\[ \] (C) Provide a pre-arrival sensory description as an accessible document that covers lighting, likely noise, smells, busy periods, quiet spaces and the appearance of the route.

## 2\. The route to where you want to get to

\[ \] (P) Provide continuous step-free routes from parking/drop-off and accommodation to all facilities, including information about travelling to rehearsal and performance spaces. Accessible routes should not be needlessly longer or segregating.

\[ \] (P/N) Doors must be wide enough for the largest powered chair to comfortably pass through (850 to 1000mm) and should be independently operable by someone with limited mobility or strength in a seated position from either a manual or powered wheelchair. Use powered doors, if possible, operated by an open/close/release button being easily identifiable and placed far enough that the door will not clash with the operator's wheelchair. Ensure the doors have a soft-close mechanism, with a closure delay and an option to easily keep them open.  
Manual powered-door controls should be 750-1000 mm above floor level; position them consistently between locations, with clear visual contrast, and they should be operable with a closed fist. Manual doors also must be wide enough for the largest powered chair to comfortably pass through.  
Doors must be identifiable using the Apple iPhone camera door identifier function.  
Wifi must be well supported to support services such as Be My Eyes.

\[ \] (P) Corridors, lobbies and turning spaces must accommodate manual and powered wheelchairs, mobility scooters and companions. Keep routes free from furniture, cables, bins and temporary equipment.

\[ \] (N) Lifts should provide spoken floor/direction information, visual indicators, NaviLens stickers and physical tactile/Braille controls. Never rely on Braille applied to a featureless touchscreen as the only control and never apply braille to touch sensitive buttons.

\[ \] (N/C) Use continuous high-contrast visual route cues where useful, especially tactile high contrast floor strips and changes in carpet patterns outside of lifts, and ugh contrast and tactile edges to steps. Any tactile guiding surface should be professionally designed; improvised raised strips can introduce trip hazards or conflict with recognised tactile paving.

\[ \] (N/P) Provide consistent controllable lighting, contrasting step nosings and handrails. Avoid glare, sudden lighting changes and confusing floor or wall patterns in general spaces.

\[ \] (C) Identify and offer a quieter, lower-stimulation route where possible and show it on maps.

\[ \] (P/N/C) Fire escape planning must include disabled occupants: accessible evacuation strategy, PEEPs where appropriate, refuges/evacuation lifts where designed, and multimodal warnings.

\[ \] (C) Fire warnings should explain the procedure and illuminate safe route to the nearest exits.

## 3\. The rooms

\[ \] (P) Doors must be wide enough for the largest powered chair to comfortably pass through (850 to 1000mm) and must be operable by someone from a seated position with limited mobility, reach or strength. Powered doors are preferable, operated by an open/close/release button and open inwards, with the control button inside the room being placed far enough from the door ensuring it will not clash with the operator's wheelchair. Ensure the doors have a soft-close mechanism, with a closure delay and an option to easily keep them open. Part M places manual powered-door controls 750-1000 mm above floor level; position them consistently, with visual contrast, outside the door swing and operable with a closed fist. The doors must be wide enough for the largest powered chair to comfortably pass through.

\[ \] (P/C) Locate accessible/sensory-friendly rooms away from lifts, plant rooms, bins, kitchens and other predictable noise or odour sources where possible.

\[ \] (P) Allow wheelchair manoeuvring, parking and safe charging without blocking escape. Provide transfer space at the bed and bathroom; offer left- and right-transfer bathroom layouts. Where practical, allow bed approach from both sides.

\[ \] (P) Do not mandate an unusually low fixed bed: transfer needs vary. Use transfer-compatible or height-adjustable beds; provide double beds if possible.

\[ \] (P) Provide nearby PA/companion rooms, ideally with a connecting door.

\[ \] (P/N) Put essential storage, switches, sockets, windows/blinds, mirrors, washbasins, toilet paper, shower controls and emergency assistance controls within seated/short-stature reach. Use lever/easy-grip fittings. Ensure toilet roll can be easily reached without unnecessary stretching or rotation.

\[ \] (C/N) Provide dimmable, flicker-controlled lighting (no neon lights) and blackout; quiet ventilation; low-glare finishes; restrained patterns; and good acoustic separation. The room should be capable of becoming a dark, quiet recovery space.

\[ \] (P/C) Provide accessible easy-grip kitchenware/utensils on request, clear circulation, reachable appliances and space for an assistance-dog bed and bowls.

\[ \] (C/N) Give each occupant an accessible room guide in plain language explaining layout, controls, emergency arrangements, routes and how to request adjustments.

\[ \] (P) Do not make safety depend on an occupant operating a fire extinguisher. Any firefighting equipment provided must be accessible, but evacuation is the priority.

## 4\. Assistive technology, alarms and commissioning

\[ \] (N) TVs and media systems should support captions, audio description, talking menus, magnification/zoom and accessible remotes, with a stand for the remote to be stored close to the TV.

\[ \] (N) Apple Magnifier should be able to detect doors, read nearby signs and describe how a door opens on supported iPhones. Treat this as a supplement only: Apple warns against relying on Detection Mode for navigation or emergencies, but it is appropriate for daily use.

\[ \] (N) Ensure cupboards, the fridge, bathroom and other key information has NaviLens stickers, and good wifi is essential for BeMyEyes.

\[ \] (P/N/C) Smart lighting, Bluetooth controls, electronic maps and voice control can add choice, but essential functions must also have simple physical controls and work if Wi-Fi, a phone or an app fails.

\[ \] (N/C) Fire warnings must remain compliant with the fire strategy. Do not simply make alarms quieter to reduce sensory shock. Provide appropriate audible, visual and vibrating/pillow-pager options, consider clear voice messages where designed, and allow advance familiarisation for sensory-sensitive occupants.

\[ \] (P) Emergency assistance alarms must be reachable from the bed, toilet, shower and floor, operable with limited dexterity and connected to a reliable response process.

\[ \] (P/N/C) Before opening, consider running full-journey walkthroughs with disabled and neurodivergent actors including manual and powered wheelchair users, mobility-scooter users, people of short stature, blind and low-vision people, Deaf/hard-of-hearing people, and autistic people with sensory sensitivities. Test daytime, night-time and emergency scenarios and fix barriers before handover.
